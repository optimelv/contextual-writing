# Professional communication

## Email and chat

Use a subject or opening that reveals purpose. Provide only the context needed for the recipient to understand and act. State the request, owner, timing, or decision explicitly when relevant. Match greeting, formality, `du` or `Sie`, and closing to the relationship and organization.

Do not add ceremonial politeness, fake warmth, an unnecessary deadline, or a menu of actions when one action is required. Use bullets only for genuinely separate items.

## Meeting follow-up

Ground the output in a transcript, notes, recording summary, or user-confirmed evidence. Extract:

- purpose and key points
- decisions and commitments
- actions, owners, and due dates
- blockers, risks, and open questions
- internal-only information that must not enter external copy

Use `unknown` or `TBD` where evidence is missing. Preserve the difference between agreed, proposed, open, and unverified. A customer-safe email must not expose internal strategy or turn seller intent into customer commitment.

## Memo and report

Open with the decision, answer, or purpose. Define scope, evidence, constraints, assumptions, and unknowns. Build each section around one analytical job. End with the decision, recommendation, or next safe action the document is meant to support.

## Technical communication

Start with the user, operational, or business outcome. State what is implemented, tested, observed, inferred, or unverified. Define inputs, outputs, dependencies, units, failure modes, and tradeoffs. Preserve code, filenames, APIs, and precise terminology.

## Administrative and legal context

Establish jurisdiction, effective date, recipient authority, relevant document, and practical objective. Separate admitted facts, disputed facts, source claims, and unknowns. Preserve operative legal terms and avoid unsupported concessions.

Use calm, precise language. Avoid performative lawyer wording, broad accusations, empty threats, and legal citations that were not verified. Acknowledge a real error when authorized and strategically appropriate. Recommend qualified legal review when rights, liability, deadlines, or material consequences are at stake.

Do not rewrite operative legal, contractual, regulatory, policy, technical specification, or compliance wording merely to sound natural. Keep the authoritative text unchanged. A plain-language explanation must be visibly separate and must state that it does not replace the operative wording.

Preserve operative triggers, thresholds, defined terms, deadlines, parties, duties, exceptions, and scope inside the explanation. When a simpler synonym could change interpretation, retain the operative term and explain it parenthetically instead of substituting another threshold, for example retain `material` rather than replacing it with `significant`.

These rules do not apply to ordinary emails merely because they are formal.
